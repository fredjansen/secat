Introduction

The field of software engineering is characterized by a fundamental dichotomy in the creation and dissemination of knowledge.

The practitioner–researcher chasm

Academic literature

Formal, rigorously peer-reviewed literature.

Produced by
Researchers
Published in
Journals, Conference proceedings
Controlled by
Academic publishers

Grey literature

Vast and dynamic body of work.

Produced by
Practitioners, Software engineers, Architects, Technical leaders
Ecosystem
Industry

Barriers between the two worlds

For practitioners

  • Academic journals behind expensive paywalls
  • Dense and theoretical prose
  • Time required to parse scientific papers

For researchers

  • View GL as non-scientific or anecdotal
  • Lack of formal independent peer-review process

Result: Chasm between state-of-the-art (academic) and state-of-the-practice (industry).

Why grey literature proliferates

Direct functional response to structural gap: necessary purpose-built infrastructure for knowledge exchange.

Practitioner needs

  • Current information
  • Practical information
  • Context-specific information

Characteristics

Formal definition

“That which is produced on all levels of government, academics, business and industry in print and electronic formats, but which is not controlled by commercial publishers.”
Luxembourg definition — established in 1997, expanded in 2004. Key criterion: lack of control by commercial publishing entities.

In the software engineering context

Grey literature is functionally defined as:

  • Any literature not subjected to formal independent peer-review process before publication
  • Material not indexed by major academic database vendors

Primary distinction between GL and white literature of academic journals and conferences. Major databases excluded: ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore.
